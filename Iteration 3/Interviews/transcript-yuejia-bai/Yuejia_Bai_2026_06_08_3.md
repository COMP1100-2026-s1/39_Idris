

**Duration:** Around 10–12 minutes
**Participant:** UQ student
**Context:** Before using the club selection website prototype

---

**Interviewer:**
Hi, thank you so much for doing this interview with me. Before we start, just to let you know, this is very casual. There are no right or wrong answers. I’m just trying to understand how students normally find clubs and how they decide whether to join one.

**Participant:**
Yeah, no worries.

**Interviewer:**
Great. So first, have you joined any UQ clubs before?

**Participant:**
Um, yes, but not many properly. I think I signed up for a few at Market Day before, but I only really went to events for one or two. For some clubs, I followed their Instagram or joined the mailing list, but then I didn’t really continue.

**Interviewer:**
Why do you think you didn’t continue?

**Participant:**
Honestly, sometimes I just forgot. At Market Day, everything feels exciting, and people explain their clubs to you. You think, “Oh yeah, this sounds fun.” But after that, uni gets busy. If the club doesn’t clearly tell me what to do next, I just don’t follow up.

**Interviewer:**
So it’s not always because you lost interest, but because the next step was unclear?

**Participant:**
Yeah, exactly. Sometimes I was still interested, but I didn’t know what I was supposed to do. Like, do I need to pay first? Do I need to join a group chat? Do I wait for an email? Can I just go to the next event? If I’m not sure, I might just leave it.

**Interviewer:**
When you usually look for clubs, where do you search?

**Participant:**
Mostly Instagram and Market Day. Sometimes friends tell me about clubs too. I know there is the UQ Union website, but I don’t use it much unless I already know the club name. It feels more like a list, not really something I browse deeply.

**Interviewer:**
What is difficult about using Instagram or other platforms?

**Participant:**
The information is kind of messy. One club has a joining link in their bio, another uses stories, another uses Facebook or Discord. If I want to compare clubs, I have to jump between different platforms. It’s not impossible, but it’s annoying.

**Interviewer:**
When you compare clubs, what are you usually comparing?

**Participant:**
Probably the events, how often they meet, whether it costs money, and whether it feels welcoming. Also whether I need experience. For example, for sports or performance clubs, I’m not sure if beginners can join or if everyone is already really good.

**Interviewer:**
Is beginner-friendliness important to you?

**Participant:**
Yeah, very important. If a club clearly says “no experience needed” or “new members welcome,” I feel much more comfortable. Otherwise I worry I’ll go there and feel like I don’t belong.

**Interviewer:**
Do you think going to a club event alone is a concern for students?

**Participant:**
Yeah, definitely. If I have a friend with me, it’s much easier. But going alone can feel socially scary. You don’t know if people will talk to you, or if everyone already has their own friend group. So even if I’m interested, I might think, “Maybe next time,” and then I never go.

**Interviewer:**
What kind of information would make you feel more comfortable before going?

**Participant:**
I want to know what actually happens at the event. Like, if it says “social night,” what does that mean? Is it dinner? Games? Just talking? Is there someone helping new people? Can I come alone? Do I need to register? Those details matter.

**Interviewer:**
Do clubs usually provide those details?

**Participant:**
Some do, but many don’t. They often say things like “come along for a fun night” or “meet new people,” which sounds nice, but it doesn’t really tell me what to expect.

**Interviewer:**
So the descriptions feel too general?

**Participant:**
Yeah, a bit. Every club says they are friendly and fun. I’m sure many of them are, but as a student trying to decide, that doesn’t help me compare. I need more specific information.

**Interviewer:**
What would a useful club description look like?

**Participant:**
Something more concrete. Like, “We run weekly board game nights, monthly dinners, and beginner workshops. Most people come casually, and you can join anytime.” That tells me much more than just saying the club is friendly.

**Interviewer:**
How important is time commitment?

**Participant:**
Very important. Uni workload changes a lot. Sometimes I have assignments and exams, so I don’t want to join something that makes me feel guilty if I miss events. If a club is casual and flexible, I want to know that. If it needs regular attendance, I also want to know before joining.

**Interviewer:**
What about cost?

**Participant:**
Cost matters too. I don’t mind paying a small fee, but I want to know upfront. Sometimes there is a membership fee, then event fees, equipment, or trips. It’s okay, but I don’t want surprises.

**Interviewer:**
Would unclear cost stop you from joining?

**Participant:**
Maybe not always, but it makes me hesitate. If one club clearly says it’s five dollars and another doesn’t say anything, I might trust the first one more.

**Interviewer:**
So transparency builds trust?

**Participant:**
Yeah, definitely. It also makes the club look more organised.

**Interviewer:**
Do you usually trust current club information?

**Participant:**
It depends. Official information is useful, but it can be outdated. Instagram is good for recent events, but it can be promotional. Friends are trustworthy, but they only know their own experience. So each source helps, but none of them gives the full picture.

**Interviewer:**
If there was one website to help students choose clubs, what would you expect it to include?

**Participant:**
Search and filters first. Then proper club profiles. I would want to see what the club does, who it’s good for, photos, event examples, cost, time commitment, joining steps, and maybe student reviews.

**Interviewer:**
Student reviews?

**Participant:**
Yeah. Real student comments would help a lot. Not long reviews, just short comments. Like, “I joined alone and people were nice,” or “good for networking but a bit formal,” or “events are fun but sometimes crowded.” That feels more honest than only official descriptions.

**Interviewer:**
Would negative or mixed reviews be useful?

**Participant:**
Yeah, if they are honest. Like, if a club is high commitment, that’s not bad, but I want to know. It might be good for someone else, just not for me. Mixed reviews actually feel more trustworthy than only positive ones.

**Interviewer:**
Would ratings help, like stars?

**Participant:**
Maybe, but I think tags and comments are more helpful. A five-star rating doesn’t tell me why. I’d rather see tags like “beginner-friendly,” “low commitment,” “social,” or “career-focused.”

**Interviewer:**
What tags would you personally look for?

**Participant:**
Beginner-friendly, low commitment, no experience needed, good for meeting friends, low cost, international-student friendly, regular events, and maybe “come alone friendly.”

**Interviewer:**
“Come alone friendly” is a good phrase. Would that make you more likely to go?

**Participant:**
Yes, 100%. Because then I know new people are expected, and I won’t feel weird showing up alone.

**Interviewer:**
Do you think club culture is easy to understand before joining?

**Participant:**
No, not really. You can guess from photos or posts, but you don’t really know. Some clubs might be very welcoming, but their online page doesn’t show it. Or some clubs look friendly online but feel different in person.

**Interviewer:**
How could a website show club culture better?

**Participant:**
Maybe through student reviews, photos, and a section like “What to expect at your first event.” For example, “committee members will welcome new people” or “there will be icebreaker games.” That would make it less scary.

**Interviewer:**
Would a comparison feature be useful?

**Participant:**
Yes, definitely. Right now comparing clubs is annoying. If I could pick two or three clubs and compare them side by side, I would use that.

**Interviewer:**
What would you want to compare?

**Participant:**
Cost, commitment, event frequency, beginner-friendliness, main activities, joining steps, and maybe student comments. Also whether the club is more social, professional, cultural, sporty, or academic.

**Interviewer:**
Would that help you choose more confidently?

**Participant:**
Yeah. Sometimes I’m interested in multiple clubs, but I only have time or money for one or two. Comparison would help me decide instead of just giving up.

**Interviewer:**
Have you ever given up because there were too many options?

**Participant:**
Yeah, especially at Market Day. You see so many clubs, and after a while, everything blends together. You sign up for some, but then you don’t remember why.

**Interviewer:**
So more options do not always help?

**Participant:**
No, not always. More options are good only if you can filter and compare them. Otherwise it’s overwhelming.

**Interviewer:**
Would you use a short recommendation quiz?

**Participant:**
Maybe, if it’s short. Like if it asks whether I want to make friends, build career skills, try a hobby, or join cultural events. But I would want it to explain why a club is recommended.

**Interviewer:**
What would make you stop using a club website?

**Participant:**
Outdated information. If the joining link doesn’t work, or the events are from last year, I would stop trusting it. Also, if it’s just a long list with vague descriptions, then it’s not really different from what already exists.

**Interviewer:**
Would a “last updated” date help?

**Participant:**
Yes, definitely. If it says updated this semester, I feel more confident.

**Interviewer:**
Who do you think would benefit most from this kind of website?

**Participant:**
First-year students, international students, and students who are shy or don’t have many friends yet. If you already have friends in clubs, it’s easier. But if you’re new, you need clearer information.

**Interviewer:**
Would you personally use a website like this?

**Participant:**
Yeah, I think I would use it at the start of semester. I probably wouldn’t use it every week, but during club sign-up time, yes. I would browse clubs, compare a few, and decide which first event to go to.

**Interviewer:**
What would make you actually attend an event?

**Participant:**
Clear information. If I know the event is beginner-friendly, I can come alone, it’s not too expensive, and I know exactly what to do, then I’m much more likely to go. Real student comments would also help.

**Interviewer:**
If you could ask every club to answer five questions clearly, what would they be?

**Participant:**
What do you actually do? Who is it for? Do I need experience? How much time or money does it take? And what are the exact joining steps?

**Interviewer:**
That’s very clear. What do you think is the biggest problem overall with how students choose clubs now?

**Participant:**
I think the biggest problem is not that clubs are hard to find. It’s that it’s hard to know which club fits you. The information is there sometimes, but it’s scattered, vague, or too promotional. So you don’t feel confident.

**Interviewer:**
Confidence seems like a big theme.

**Participant:**
Yeah. Joining a club is social. You have to show up, meet people, spend time, maybe spend money. So you want to feel like it’s worth it before you go.

**Interviewer:**
Before we finish, is there anything else you want to add?

**Participant:**
Maybe just that clubs are really important for uni life, but starting is hard. A lot of students want to join something, but they don’t know what suits them, or they feel nervous. So if a website can make the information clearer and make the first step less scary, I think it would be useful.

**Interviewer:**
Thank you so much. That was really helpful.

**Participant:**
No worries. Hope that helps.
