

Duration: Around 10–12 minutes
Participant: UQ student
Context: Before using the club selection website prototype

⸻

Interviewer:
Hi, thanks for doing this interview with me. This is just a casual chat, so don’t worry about giving perfect answers. I’m mainly trying to understand how students find clubs and what makes them decide to join or not join.

Participant:
Yeah, sure. That’s fine.

Interviewer:
Great. So first, have you joined any UQ clubs before?

Participant:
Yeah, I have, but not a lot. I think I joined a couple before, and I signed up for a few more at Market Day. But to be honest, some of them I just signed up and then never really went to anything.

Interviewer:
Why do you think that happened?

Participant:
I think because at Market Day everything feels really exciting. Everyone is talking to you, giving you flyers, saying their club is fun. So I sign up because I feel interested at that moment. But later, when I actually go back to uni work, I kind of forget. Also, sometimes I don’t really know what to do next.

Interviewer:
What do you mean by “what to do next”?

Participant:
Like, after I sign up, I don’t always know if I need to pay a membership fee, or join a Discord, or wait for an email, or just go to an event. Sometimes the information is in different places, so I just don’t follow up.

Interviewer:
So it’s not always because you don’t like the club anymore?

Participant:
Yeah, not really. Sometimes I still think the club sounds interesting, but if the next step is not clear, I just leave it. Especially when I’m busy, I don’t want to spend too much time searching.

Interviewer:
Where do you usually look for club information?

Participant:
Mostly Instagram. Also friends. And Market Day at the start of semester. I know there is the UQ Union website, but I don’t use it that much. It feels more like a directory. Like, it tells me the club exists, but I still need to go somewhere else to see what the club is actually like.

Interviewer:
Why do you prefer Instagram?

Participant:
Because Instagram feels more real, I guess. You can see photos, events, stories, who went there, that kind of thing. It gives you a better feeling of the club. But Instagram is also messy, because not every club posts clearly.

Interviewer:
Messy in what way?

Participant:
Like some clubs post a lot, some barely post. Some put important links in their bio, some put them in stories, some use Facebook, some use Discord. So if I want to compare clubs, I have to jump around everywhere. It’s kind of annoying.

Interviewer:
When you compare clubs, what are you usually looking at?

Participant:
I look at what events they do, how active they are, whether it costs money, and whether it seems welcoming. I also care about whether beginners can join. Because sometimes I’m interested in something, but I’m not sure if I need experience.

Interviewer:
Can you give an example?

Participant:
Like a sports club or a dance club. I might think it looks fun, but then I wonder if everyone there is already good. If I’m a beginner, I don’t want to feel embarrassed. So if the club clearly says “no experience needed,” that helps a lot.

Interviewer:
Would that make you more likely to join?

Participant:
Yeah, definitely. Even just seeing “beginners welcome” makes me feel less nervous. It tells me I won’t be the only one who doesn’t know what to do.

Interviewer:
Do you ever feel nervous about going to a club event alone?

Participant:
Yes, a lot. I think that’s actually one of the biggest things. If I have a friend going with me, I’m way more likely to go. But if I have to go alone, I start thinking, what if everyone already knows each other? What if I just stand there awkwardly? So sometimes I don’t go.

Interviewer:
So even if you are interested, that nervousness can stop you?

Participant:
Yeah. I might tell myself I’ll go next time, but then next time I’m still nervous, or I forget. So yeah, the first step is hard.

Interviewer:
What would make the first step easier?

Participant:
Probably clear information about what the event is like. Like, if it says “social night,” that’s still too vague. I want to know, is it dinner? Games? Talking? Is there a plan? Are committee members helping new people? Can I come alone? Do I need to register?

Interviewer:
Do clubs usually give that level of detail?

Participant:
Some do, but a lot don’t. Many just write something like “come meet new people and have fun.” That sounds nice, but it doesn’t really answer my questions.

Interviewer:
So you want more practical details?

Participant:
Yeah, exactly. Not just promotional words. I don’t need the club to keep saying it’s amazing. I want to know what actually happens and whether it suits me.

Interviewer:
What does “suits me” mean when you think about clubs?

Participant:
I guess it means the club fits my personality, my schedule, and what I’m looking for. Like sometimes I want a chill social club where I can make friends. Sometimes maybe I want something career-related. Sometimes I just want a hobby. So I need to know what the club is really for.

Interviewer:
Is that easy to tell from club pages now?

Participant:
Not always. A lot of descriptions are very general. Like “we connect students with similar interests.” That doesn’t tell me much. I want examples, like “we do weekly board game nights,” or “we run monthly networking events,” or “we have beginner workshops.” That’s much clearer.

Interviewer:
How important is time commitment to you?

Participant:
Pretty important. Uni gets busy very quickly. If a club has events every week, I want to know whether I’m expected to go every week or if I can just come when I’m free. I don’t want to join and then feel guilty for not attending.

Interviewer:
So flexibility matters?

Participant:
Yeah, definitely. I think if a club says “casual attendance is fine,” I would feel more comfortable. But if it needs regular commitment, that’s okay too, I just want to know before joining.

Interviewer:
What about cost?

Participant:
Cost matters too. I don’t mind paying a small membership fee, but I want it to be clear. Sometimes there might be extra costs for events, trips, equipment, or food. If that’s not clear, I might hesitate.

Interviewer:
Would unclear cost make you trust the club less?

Participant:
Maybe a little. Like if one club clearly says the membership is five dollars and events are usually free, and another club doesn’t say anything, I feel more confident with the first one. It just feels more organised.

Interviewer:
Do you usually trust the information clubs provide online?

Participant:
Kind of, but not fully. Official pages feel more reliable, but sometimes they are outdated. Instagram feels current, but it can be very promotional. Friends feel honest, but they only know their own experience. So I usually need to check multiple places.

Interviewer:
Would student reviews help?

Participant:
Yes, I think so. Not like long reviews, but short real comments. Like, “I joined alone and people were friendly,” or “good club, but events can get crowded,” or “great for networking, but more formal.” That would help me understand the club better.

Interviewer:
Would negative comments put you off?

Participant:
Not always. I think honest comments are better. If someone says a club is high commitment, that doesn’t mean it’s bad. It just means I can decide if it works for me. If everything is only positive, it feels a bit fake.

Interviewer:
So balanced information feels more trustworthy?

Participant:
Yeah. I don’t expect every club to be perfect. I just want to know what to expect.

Interviewer:
Would ratings help? Like five-star ratings?

Participant:
Maybe a little, but I think comments and tags are better. A star rating doesn’t tell me why someone liked or disliked the club. Tags are easier to understand quickly.

Interviewer:
What kind of tags would you want to see?

Participant:
Beginner-friendly, low commitment, no experience needed, good for meeting friends, low cost, regular events, career-focused, and maybe “come alone friendly.”

Interviewer:
“Come alone friendly” is interesting. Why is that important?

Participant:
Because people don’t always have someone to go with. If I see that tag, I feel like the club expects new people and I won’t be the only one coming alone. That would make me much more likely to try it.

Interviewer:
Do you think club culture is easy to understand before joining?

Participant:
No, not really. You can guess from photos and posts, but you don’t really know until you go. Some clubs might be really welcoming in real life, but their online page doesn’t show that. Or sometimes the page looks friendly, but the event feels different.

Interviewer:
How could a website show club culture better?

Participant:
Maybe through photos, student comments, and something like “what to expect at your first event.” That would be really helpful. Like, if it says committee members will welcome new students, or there are icebreaker games, I feel less worried.

Interviewer:
Would you use a website that lets you compare clubs side by side?

Participant:
Yes, I think that would be really useful. Because right now, comparing clubs is mostly in my head. I open a few Instagram pages, then I forget which club had what. A comparison table would make it easier.

Interviewer:
What would you compare?

Participant:
Cost, time commitment, event frequency, beginner-friendliness, main activities, joining steps, and maybe student comments. Also whether the club is more social, professional, cultural, sporty, or academic.

Interviewer:
Would that help you make a decision?

Participant:
Yeah. Sometimes I’m interested in a few clubs, but I only have time for maybe one or two. If I can compare them clearly, I’m less likely to just give up.

Interviewer:
Have you ever felt overwhelmed by too many clubs?

Participant:
Yes, especially at Market Day. There are so many clubs, and after a while they all start to sound similar. I might sign up for a few, but later I don’t remember which one I actually liked.

Interviewer:
So more choices don’t always make it easier?

Participant:
No, not really. More choices are only helpful if I can filter them. Otherwise it just becomes too much.

Interviewer:
Would a short recommendation quiz be useful?

Participant:
Maybe, if it’s actually short. Like, asking if I want to make friends, learn a skill, build my career, or join cultural events. Then it can suggest clubs. But I would want it to explain why those clubs are recommended.

Interviewer:
Why is the explanation important?

Participant:
Because otherwise it feels random. If it says, “recommended because you selected low commitment and social events,” then I understand the reason and trust it more.

Interviewer:
What would make you stop using a club website?

Participant:
Outdated information. If the event dates are old or the joining link doesn’t work, I would stop trusting it. Also, if it’s just another long list of clubs with vague descriptions, then it doesn’t really solve anything.

Interviewer:
Would a “last updated” date help?

Participant:
Yes, definitely. If I see it was updated this semester, I feel more confident that the information is real.

Interviewer:
Who do you think would benefit most from this kind of website?

Participant:
Probably first-year students, international students, and students who are shy or don’t have many friends yet. If you already have friends in clubs, it’s easier. But if you’re new, clearer information really helps.

Interviewer:
Would you personally use this kind of website?

Participant:
Yeah, especially at the start of semester. I probably wouldn’t use it every week, but during club sign-up time I would use it to browse, compare, and decide which event to try first.

Interviewer:
What would make you actually attend an event?

Participant:
If I know it’s beginner-friendly, I can come alone, the cost is clear, and I know exactly what to do. Also, if I see real student comments saying it’s welcoming, I would feel much better.

Interviewer:
If every club had to answer five questions clearly, what should they answer?

Participant:
What do you actually do at events? Who is this club for? Do I need experience? How much time or money does it take? And how do I join?

Interviewer:
That’s very clear. What do you think is the biggest problem with choosing clubs now?

Participant:
I think the biggest problem is not finding clubs. It’s knowing which club actually fits you. The information is often scattered, vague, or too promotional. So you don’t feel confident enough to choose.

Interviewer:
That’s really helpful. Anything else you want to add?

Participant:
Maybe just that clubs are really important for uni life, but starting can be hard. A lot of students probably want to join something, but they feel nervous or confused. So if a website can make the information clearer and the first step less scary, I think it would be useful.

Interviewer:
Thank you so much. That was really helpful.

Participant:
No worries. Hope it helps.
